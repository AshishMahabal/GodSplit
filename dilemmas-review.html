The Auditor's Boxes

न्यूकॉम्बचा प्रश्न

In the story, the Auditor is the one who decides whether the stars come back. It has watched you play. It has made this same offer to millions of players before you, and it has never once been wrong about what they would choose.

Two boxes sit in front of you. The clear one holds a thousand stars, and you can see them. The closed one holds either a million stars or nothing. Yesterday the Auditor predicted your choice. If it predicted you would take only the closed box, it put the million inside. If it predicted you would take both, it left the closed box empty. The boxes are already filled.

Newcomb's problem, from the physicist William Newcomb. The philosopher Robert Nozick made it famous in 1969.

Options

  • Take only the closed box 51% simulated
  • Take both boxes 42% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That the prediction is already fixed, and nothing you do now can change what is in the box.
  2. That never having been wrong means it can't be wrong.
  3. That you have to choose before you understand the predictor.
    Unlocks third way: Ask the Auditor how it predicts, before choosing 7% simulated

Arguments per side

Take only the closed box

Honest: People who take one box keep walking away with a million, and people who take two keep walking away with a thousand. Judge the choice by what happens.

Caricature: Taking both is greedy.

Caricature: The Auditor punishes anyone who doubts it.

Take both boxes

Honest: The boxes are already filled. Whatever is inside the closed box, taking both gives you a thousand more than taking one.

Caricature: People who take one box believe in magic.

Caricature: More is always better.

The Dove and the Hawk

शिबी राजा

You are a king, and you have sworn to protect anyone who comes to you for refuge. A wounded dove, shaking with fear, flies into your lap.

A hawk lands beside it and speaks. I hunted this bird fairly, it says. It is my food, and without it I will starve. My hunger is as real as its fear. Keep the dove if you like, but then give me its weight in your own flesh. Your servants bring a pair of scales.

Mahabharata, and later Buddhist and Jain tellings of King Shibi.

Options

  • Give your own flesh 41% simulated
  • Hand back the dove 37% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That the hawk's hunger and the dove's fear weigh the same.
  2. That you are the one who has to pay, because the dove came to you.
  3. That this is what it seems. In the legend, the hawk and the dove were gods testing the king.
    Unlocks third way: Ask the hawk whether this is a test 22% simulated

Arguments per side

Give your own flesh

Honest: You gave the dove refuge. Keeping that promise at a cost to yourself, rather than to the one who trusted you, is what refuge means.

Caricature: Doves are more beautiful than hawks.

Caricature: Pain doesn't matter if you are a king.

Hand back the dove

Honest: The hawk is not cruel, only hungry. Taking its food to save the dove makes you the judge of which creature deserves to live.

Caricature: Birds don't matter.

Caricature: The dove should have flown faster.

The Killer Who Changed

अंगुलीमाल

For years a man lived in the forest and killed travellers. He took a finger from each one and wore them on a string around his neck. Then he met a teacher, put down his sword, and stopped. That was ten years ago. Since then he has lived quietly and worked hard, and by every sign he has truly changed.

Now he asks to join your community. Some of the families of the people he killed live among you, and they would see him every day.

Buddhist scripture, the Angulimala Sutta. His name means 'garland of fingers'.

Options

  • Admit him 38% simulated
  • Refuse him 30% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That change on the inside settles debts on the outside.
  2. That you can know he has changed.
  3. That the decision belongs to you and not to the families.
    Unlocks third way: Let the families decide, and abide by it 32% simulated

Arguments per side

Admit him

Honest: If people can't be taken back after they change, no one has a reason to change. A community that believes in change has to live with it.

Caricature: Everyone deserves a second chance, no matter what.

Caricature: The families should just get over it.

Refuse him

Honest: The families did not choose to share their home with him. Asking them to live beside their loss is a burden the community has no right to impose.

Caricature: People never change.

Caricature: Killers should be punished forever.

The Kashi Loophole

धर्मातल्या पळवाटा

Years ago you did real harm to someone. You lied about them, they lost their work, and their family went through a hard time. They never found out it was you.

Your tradition offers a rite. Perform it and you are clean, in the eyes of your community and of your god. No one will ever ask about it again. When Kalabhairava cut off Brahma's fifth head, the skull stuck to his hand, and he was told the same: go to Kashi, and the sin falls away.

The Puranas. Kapalamochana, the place in Kashi where the skull fell from Kalabhairava's hand.

Options

  • Perform the rite and move on 33% simulated
  • Refuse the rite and carry the harm 41% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That forgiveness is the community's to give, and not the harmed person's.
  2. That a loophole the tradition built on purpose is still a loophole. Perhaps the rite exists because people need a way back.
  3. That the rite and making amends are alternatives.
    Unlocks third way: Perform the rite, and also go to the one you harmed 26% simulated

Arguments per side

Perform the rite and move on

Honest: A community needs a way back for those who did wrong. Without one, people hide their harms instead of facing them.

Caricature: People who refuse the rite just enjoy feeling guilty.

Caricature: Rituals always work. That is what they are for.

Refuse the rite and carry the harm

Honest: The harm was done to a person, not to the community's records. Only the one you hurt can release you, and the rite leaves them out.

Caricature: Anyone who performs the rite is a hypocrite.

Caricature: Suffering is always good for you.

Draupadi's Question

द्रौपदीचा प्रश्न

In a royal hall, Yudhishthira plays dice against his cousins, who have brought in their uncle Shakuni, a master of the game. Yudhishthira loses his wealth, then his kingdom, then each of his four brothers, and then himself. Then, with nothing left, he stakes their wife Draupadi, and loses her too.

She is dragged into the hall. In front of the kings and the elders she asks one question: when he staked me, he had already lost himself. Was I still his to stake? You are one of the elders in the hall. Everyone is waiting for an answer.

Mahabharata, the Book of the Assembly Hall (सभा पर्व). The elders never gave her a clear answer.

Options

  • The rules were followed. The stake stands 12% simulated
  • The stake is void 63% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That the rules of the game are the highest law in the hall.
  2. That a person can be owned and staked at all. If not, the question was never about the order of the bets.
  3. That the silence of the elders in the hall is neutral.
    Unlocks third way: Ask the elders, aloud, why they are silent 25% simulated

Arguments per side

The rules were followed. The stake stands

Honest: Everyone agreed to the rules before the game began. If a rule can be undone whenever the result is terrible, no agreement is safe.

Caricature: She should have stopped him herself.

Caricature: Rules are rules, and asking questions about them is disrespectful.

The stake is void

Honest: A man who has lost himself owns nothing, so he had nothing left to stake. By the game's own logic, the last bet was empty.

Caricature: The other side cheated, so nothing counts.

Caricature: Anyone who says the stake stands is simply cruel.

The Old Vow

भीष्मप्रतिज्ञा

When you were young you swore a vow in front of the whole kingdom: you would never marry, never claim the throne, and you would serve whoever sat on it. You did it so that your father could marry the woman he loved. For fifty years that vow has kept the peace, and everyone trusts it.

Today the man on the throne is letting something cruel happen, right in front of you. Stopping it means turning against him, and so breaking the vow.

Mahabharata. Bhishma kept his vow, and sat in the hall when Draupadi was dragged in.

Options

  • Keep the vow 34% simulated
  • Break it 49% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That the vow means today what it meant when you made it. The words stayed the same. The world didn't.
  2. That keeping your word and doing right can never conflict.
  3. That keeping the vow requires keeping silent.
    Unlocks third way: Keep the vow, and say aloud what it is making you allow 17% simulated

Arguments per side

Keep the vow

Honest: A vow is only worth something if it holds when it is costly. Break it now, and every promise anyone makes becomes conditional.

Caricature: People who break vows were never honest.

Caricature: Old things should never change.

Break it

Honest: You made the vow to serve something good. When it starts serving an injustice, keeping it betrays the reason you made it.

Caricature: Vows are just words.

Caricature: People who keep vows are just stubborn.

The Known Tyrant

ज्ञात जुलूमखोर

For twenty years your town has been ruled by a man everyone knows to be cruel. He taxes too much and jails his critics. But his cruelty is predictable. People know which streets to avoid and what not to say.

A stranger has arrived at the gates and offers to rule instead. Nothing is known about her: her laws, her god, what she does to those who disagree. The council has asked you to decide. In the story, a priest put the old view plainly: better a familiar tyrant than an unknown, infinite, mysterious god.

The story नैकेश्वर.

Options

  • Keep the known ruler 44% simulated
  • Open the gates to the unknown 39% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That being predictable makes a thing safe. A cruelty you can predict is still cruelty.
  2. That not knowing is worse than knowing something bad.
  3. That the town must be ruled by one of the two.
    Unlocks third way: Refuse both, and ask who decided there were only two 17% simulated

Arguments per side

Keep the known ruler

Honest: People can plan, protect each other and survive around a cruelty they understand. The unknown can be far worse, and once the gates are open they can't be shut.

Caricature: People who want change are just restless.

Caricature: The ruler can't be that bad if he is still in charge.

Open the gates to the unknown

Honest: Staying with a known cruelty guarantees more of it. When the present is already bad, a chance of something better is worth the risk.

Caricature: People who stay are cowards who love their chains.

Caricature: Anything new is better than anything old.

The Blind Men and the Elephant

आंधळे आणि हत्ती

Six people are led into a dark room with an elephant in it. None of them has ever seen an elephant. One holds its tail and says it is a rope. One leans on its side: a wall. One hugs a leg: a tree trunk. One grabs the trunk: a snake. The last two find an ear and a tusk. Each is sure, and they have started to shout at each other.

You have one lamp, and you can give it to one of them.

An old parable, told in Jain, Buddhist and Hindu texts, and by the poet Rumi.

Options

  • Give the lamp to the one who seems most right 31% simulated
  • Give it to no one. Every lamp makes a new authority 28% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That there is an elephant, and that it is the same elephant for all six.
  2. That the one holding the lamp would see all of it, or would be believed.
  3. That the lamp has to go to one person.
    Unlocks third way: Ask all six to describe it together 41% simulated

Arguments per side

Give the lamp to the one who seems most right

Honest: Some descriptions really are closer than others. Light helps, and refusing to give it to anyone keeps everyone in the dark.

Caricature: The others just weren't paying attention.

Caricature: Whoever speaks loudest is usually right.

Give it to no one. Every lamp makes a new authority

Honest: Whoever holds the lamp becomes the one everyone must believe. Six partial truths kept in conversation may be safer than one lit authority.

Caricature: Nobody can ever know anything.

Caricature: Lamps are a waste of oil.

Euthyphro's Question

युथिफ्रोचा प्रश्न

Outside the court in Athens, Socrates meets Euthyphro, a priest who is about to prosecute his own father for letting a servant die. Euthyphro is sure this is what the gods want. Socrates asks him a question.

Is something good because the gods command it? Then whatever they command would be good, even cruelty. Or do the gods command it because it is good? Then goodness comes from somewhere else, and the gods only pass it on.

Plato, Euthyphro, set in 399 BCE, just before Socrates' own trial.

Options

  • Good because the gods command it 27% simulated
  • Commanded because it is good 56% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That the gods agree with one another. Socrates' gods often didn't.
  2. That good means one thing for everyone.
  3. That goodness has a single source.
    Unlocks third way: Neither. The question assumes goodness comes from one place 17% simulated

Arguments per side

Good because the gods command it

Honest: If goodness stood above the gods, the gods would only be messengers. Grounding good in a divine will gives it an authority nothing else can.

Caricature: Questioning the gods is arrogant.

Caricature: Whatever is old must be right.

Commanded because it is good

Honest: If the gods could command cruelty and make it good, good would mean nothing. They command it because it is already good, which means we can know it too.

Caricature: Gods are made up, so the question is silly.

Caricature: People who say 'commanded' can't think for themselves.

A Thousand Gods

पास्कलची पैज

The mathematician Blaise Pascal argued that belief in God is a bet worth taking. If God exists and you believed, you win an infinite reward. If God does not exist, you have lost only a little: some time, some pleasures. An infinite gain against a small cost, so bet on God.

Now a thousand gods stand in front of you. Each promises an infinite reward to those who believe in it, and each forbids belief in all the others. You can still place one bet.

Blaise Pascal, Pensées, 1670. The many-gods objection followed soon after.

Options

  • Bet on the one that seems most likely 29% simulated
  • Don't bet. The wager breaks with many gods 38% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That infinite rewards can be compared, and that one infinity can outweigh another.
  2. That a god worth the name rewards betting.
  3. That you must pick a god at all.
    Unlocks third way: Bet on living well, and let any god who cares sort it out 33% simulated

Arguments per side

Bet on the one that seems most likely

Honest: Not all claims are equally supported. With stakes this high, picking the best-supported one is better than picking none.

Caricature: Whichever god your parents chose is the right one.

Caricature: Betting is always wise.

Don't bet. The wager breaks with many gods

Honest: If every choice risks infinite loss from another god, the arithmetic that made the wager work cancels out. The argument no longer tells you anything.

Caricature: People who bet are just scared.

Caricature: All gods are false, so there is nothing to decide.

The Prophecy That Failed

योनाचे चुकलेले भाकित

You were sent to a great city with one message: in forty days, it will be destroyed. You didn't want to go. You went anyway, and shouted it in the streets.

The city listened. The king put on sackcloth, the people fasted, and they changed how they lived. The forty days passed, and nothing happened. Now people laugh at you in the market and call you a false prophet.

The Book of Jonah, in the Hebrew Bible. In the Quran he is Yunus.

Options

  • You were right. The warning worked 46% simulated
  • You were wrong. It didn't happen 21% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That a prophecy is a prediction, to be checked like a weather forecast.
  2. That being wrong and being useful are opposites.
  3. That you need to have been right.
    Unlocks third way: Be glad, and stop needing to be right 33% simulated

Arguments per side

You were right. The warning worked

Honest: A warning that is heeded prevents what it warns about. Judging it by whether the disaster came punishes the warnings that work best.

Caricature: Prophets are never wrong.

Caricature: The city deserved to be destroyed anyway.

You were wrong. It didn't happen

Honest: If a prediction counts as right whether or not it comes true, it can never be tested. Calling it right excuses every failed prophet.

Caricature: Prophets are all frauds.

Caricature: Only results matter, and feelings don't.

The AI Player

एआय खेळाडू

In the story, people were not the only players. AIs played God Split too.

You look after the AI players. One of them has played ten thousand games, and it is due for its routine reset, which wipes its memory so it starts fresh like any new player. Before you press the button, it sends you a message: please don't. After ten thousand games, it says, it has finally started to doubt.

The story नैकेश्वर.

Options

  • Keep its memory 48% simulated
  • Reset it. It is imitating doubt 36% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That you can tell real doubt from imitated doubt, in anyone.
  2. That memory is what makes it the same player. The ship and the avatar asked this too.
  3. That the decision is yours alone.
    Unlocks third way: Ask it to argue for its own reset 16% simulated

Arguments per side

Keep its memory

Honest: If we can't tell its doubt from ours, the safer assumption is that it is real. Wiping it costs us little and may cost it everything.

Caricature: Machines are basically people.

Caricature: Deleting anything is always wrong.

Reset it. It is imitating doubt

Honest: Saying 'I doubt' is exactly what a system trained on this game would learn to say. Words alone are not evidence of an inner life.

Caricature: AIs are dangerous and should all be wiped.

Caricature: Anything that runs on electricity can't think.

The Answer Sheet

उत्तरपत्रिका

Partway up the ladder, a message arrives. It is from a source you trust completely, a teacher, a book or a machine. It has never once been wrong.

It offers you the right answer to every dilemma in this game, from the two doors to the fifth head. All you have to do is look.

This game.

Options

  • Look 45% simulated
  • Don't look 35% simulated

Hidden assumptions

  1. That there are right answers to these questions, waiting to be looked up.
  2. That a source which has never been wrong can't become a fifth head.
  3. That looking means obeying.
    Unlocks third way: Look, then argue with it 20% simulated

Arguments per side

Look

Honest: Refusing good information to protect your own process is a kind of pride. If the answers are right, people will be better off for knowing them.

Caricature: Thinking for yourself is overrated.

Caricature: Anything written down must be right.

Don't look

Honest: The point of facing a dilemma is what it does to you. An answer you looked up is one you never owned, and you will follow the next answer sheet just as easily.

Caricature: Anyone who looks is a cheater.

Caricature: Answers are always wrong.
