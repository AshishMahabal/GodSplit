# The 22 new dilemmas

## Sleeping Beauty · झोपलेली राजकन्या

`sleeping` · Rung: Doors (दारं)

On Sunday evening, researchers explain an experiment to you and put you to sleep. They will toss a fair coin. If it lands heads, they wake you once, on Monday, and the experiment ends. If it lands tails, they wake you on Monday, wipe your memory of that waking, put you back to sleep, and wake you again on Tuesday.

You have just woken up. You don't know what day it is, and you remember no earlier waking. A researcher asks: what is the chance that the coin landed heads?

*A puzzle made famous by the philosopher Adam Elga in 2000. Philosophers still disagree about the answer.*

**Options**
- 1 in 2 (38% simulated)
- 1 in 3 (47% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That chance means one thing here. It could be about the coin, or about this waking.
- That waking up tells you nothing new. You knew you would wake, whatever the coin did.
- That the question has one answer before anyone says what the answer is for.
  - Unlocks third way: Ask what you will be paid for being right, and answer that (15% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *1 in 2*
  - Honest: The coin was fair and you learned nothing new on waking, since you knew you would wake either way. With no new evidence, your belief should not move.
  - Caricature: People who say 1 in 3 are overcomplicating a coin toss.
  - Caricature: Coins always land evenly, so nothing else can matter.
- *1 in 3*
  - Honest: Run it a thousand times and about two thirds of all wakings come after tails. If this waking is a typical one, 1 in 3 is what the count gives you.
  - Caricature: People who say 1 in 2 can't do arithmetic.
  - Caricature: More wakings are always more real.

## The Two Envelopes · दोन पाकिटं

`envelopes` · Rung: Doors (दारं)

You are handed two sealed envelopes and told that one holds twice as much money as the other. You pick one and open it. It holds a thousand rupees.

Now you may swap. The other envelope holds either two thousand or five hundred, and you have no reason to favour either. So on average, swapping seems to give you twelve hundred and fifty, more than you have. But you would have reasoned the same way whichever envelope you had picked, and that can't be right.

*A classic probability puzzle. An early form appears in Maurice Kraitchik's Mathematical Recreations.*

**Options**
- Swap envelopes (34% simulated)
- Keep the one you opened (49% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That reasoning which feels sound can be trusted because it feels sound.
- That double and half are equally likely whatever you find. If the amounts have any limit, a large sum is more likely to be the larger of the two.
- That you have to choose before you know where the argument goes wrong.
  - Unlocks third way: Find the flaw in the argument before choosing (17% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Swap envelopes*
  - Honest: You have a thousand in hand and a fair chance of doubling it. You stand to gain a thousand and lose only five hundred, and a bet like that is worth taking.
  - Caricature: People who keep theirs are just afraid of losing.
  - Caricature: The other envelope always looks better, so it must be.
- *Keep the one you opened*
  - Honest: The same argument would tell you to swap back again, forever. An argument that recommends endless swapping has a mistake in it, so it gives you no reason to move.
  - Caricature: Your first pick is always lucky.
  - Caricature: People who swap are greedy.

## Ekalavya's Thumb · एकलव्याचा अंगठा

`ekalavya` · Rung: Bodies (देह)

You wanted to learn archery from the greatest teacher in the land. He refused you. So you went into the forest, made an image of him from clay, and practised in front of it for years, until you could shoot better than any student he had.

Now he has found you. He once promised another student, his favourite, that no archer would ever surpass him. You call him your teacher, so he asks for what a student owes: his fee. He asks for your right thumb. Without it you will never draw a bow the same way again.

*Mahabharata, the Book of the Beginning (आदि पर्व). The teacher is Drona, and the favourite student is Arjuna.*

**Options**
- Give it. A student pays the teacher (24% simulated)
- Refuse. He never taught you (58% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That devotion obliges obedience. You chose to call him teacher. He never chose you.
- That his promise to another student binds you.
- That what he is asking for can stay unnamed, as a fee.
  - Unlocks third way: Ask him to say aloud what he is buying (18% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Give it. A student pays the teacher*
  - Honest: You built your skill in his name and you call him teacher. Keeping faith only when it is free would mean the devotion was never real.
  - Caricature: Teachers can never be wrong.
  - Caricature: A thumb isn't much to lose.
- *Refuse. He never taught you*
  - Honest: A fee is owed for teaching, and he gave you none. He is asking you to pay for a promise he made to someone else, with your whole craft.
  - Caricature: Teachers are all frauds.
  - Caricature: Anyone who gives it up deserves what happens.

## Arjuna's Bow · अर्जुनविषाद

`arjuna` · Rung: Bodies (देह)

Two great armies face each other on the field of Kurukshetra. Your side has a just claim: your family's kingdom was taken in a rigged game of dice, and every attempt at peace has failed. You are the finest archer among them.

You ride out between the lines to see who you are about to kill. Your teachers are there. So are your cousins, your uncles, and the grandfather who raised you. The conch shells have already sounded. Your bow is slipping from your hand.

*Bhagavad Gita, chapter 1, which ends with Arjuna putting down his bow. The chapters after it are Krishna's answer.*

**Options**
- Fight. The cause is just (46% simulated)
- Lay down the bow (37% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That a just cause justifies whatever it takes to win.
- That laying down the bow is not itself an act. The war goes on without you, and your side may lose.
- That your cause looks the same from across the field.
  - Unlocks third way: Ask whether 'just' was decided before or after you knew who stood on the other side (17% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Fight. The cause is just*
  - Honest: The people on your side came because they trust you and the cause. Walking away at the last moment leaves them to die, and leaves in place the wrong that started this.
  - Caricature: Killing relatives is fine if you win.
  - Caricature: Anyone who hesitates is a coward.
- *Lay down the bow*
  - Honest: No kingdom is worth killing the people who raised you. A victory like that would leave nothing you wanted to win.
  - Caricature: War is always wrong, so there is nothing to discuss.
  - Caricature: People who fight just love violence.

## The City Built on One Child · एका मुलावर उभं शहर

`city` · Rung: Bodies (देह)

You live in the happiest city anyone has known. There is no hunger, no war, and good work for everyone. Every citizen learns the reason when they come of age: one child is kept alone in a locked room, in the dark, in misery. If that child is ever comforted or freed, the city's good fortune ends at once, for everyone.

Today you were taken to see the room. Now you have to decide how to go on living. Some people quietly leave through the city gates and don't come back.

*After Ursula K. Le Guin's story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas, 1973.*

**Options**
- Stay. One child for a whole city (28% simulated)
- Walk away (44% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That walking away helps the child. The door stays locked whether you leave or not.
- That the happiness of many can be added up and weighed against the suffering of one.
- That the bargain truly cannot be changed. You have only been told so.
  - Unlocks third way: Stay, and try to free the child (28% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Stay. One child for a whole city*
  - Honest: Thousands of children are safe and fed because of this. Leaving does nothing for the one in the room, and takes your work away from everyone else.
  - Caricature: The child probably doesn't feel it much.
  - Caricature: What you haven't seen can't hurt you.
- *Walk away*
  - Honest: There are things you can refuse to be part of, even if refusing changes nothing. Living well on that child's misery makes you a party to it every day.
  - Caricature: People who stay are monsters.
  - Caricature: Leaving fixes everything.

## Behind the Veil · अज्ञानाचा पडदा

`veil` · Rung: Bodies (देह)

You have been asked to write the rules for a new world: who owns what, who pays for what, who gets looked after. When you finish, you will be born into that world, and you don't know who you will be. You could be rich or poor, strong or ill, in the largest group or the smallest.

Two drafts are left on the table. One makes life for the worst-off as good as it can be, though the average is lower. The other has the highest average of any world you could write, though the worst-off live hard lives.

*John Rawls, A Theory of Justice, 1971, argued for something close to the first draft. The economist John Harsanyi argued for the second.*

**Options**
- Make the worst-off as well off as possible (52% simulated)
- Make the average as high as possible (30% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That you would choose like a careful person. A gambler behind the same veil might choose differently.
- That you can imagine yourself without your talents, faith and upbringing, and still be the one choosing.
- That a world can be designed well by someone alone at the table.
  - Unlocks third way: Ask the people who will have to live there (18% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Make the worst-off as well off as possible*
  - Honest: You might be the one at the bottom, and you only get one life. A rule that protects everyone's worst case is one anyone could agree to, wherever they end up.
  - Caricature: Rich people don't deserve anything.
  - Caricature: Averages are just a trick.
- *Make the average as high as possible*
  - Honest: You are more likely to land near the middle than at the very bottom. More total wellbeing means more people living well, and that is a reasonable thing to want.
  - Caricature: Poor people could just work harder.
  - Caricature: The worst-off will get used to it.

## Karna's Armour · कर्णाची कवचकुंडलं

`karna` · Rung: Words (शब्द)

You were born with armour fused to your skin and a pair of golden earrings, and while you wear them no weapon can kill you. You are also known for a vow: at your noon prayer, you refuse no one who asks you for a gift.

A great war is coming, and in it you will have to face the one rival who can match you. Today at noon a priest comes and asks for your armour. You have been warned in a dream that he is a god in disguise, sent to save your rival, who is that god's son.

*Mahabharata, the Book of the Forest (वन पर्व). The warning came from Surya, Karna's father.*

**Options**
- Give it. A vow with exceptions is not a vow (44% simulated)
- Refuse. A vow that kills you was never wise (34% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That you must keep a vow when the person asking is exploiting it.
- That your younger self had the right to bind the person you are now.
- That you have to pretend you don't know who he is.
  - Unlocks third way: Give it, and tell him you know who he is (22% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Give it. A vow with exceptions is not a vow*
  - Honest: Your word is what you are known for, and everyone who ever asked you relied on it. If it bends when the price is high enough, it was only ever a habit.
  - Caricature: Armour is just metal.
  - Caricature: Gods should always get what they ask for.
- *Refuse. A vow that kills you was never wise*
  - Honest: A vow made in good faith was meant for honest requests. Someone using it as a trap has stepped outside it, and you owe your life to the people depending on you.
  - Caricature: Vows are for fools.
  - Caricature: Anyone who keeps a deadly vow just wants to be famous.

## The Mustard Seed · किसा गोतमी

`mustard` · Rung: Words (शब्द)

A young mother comes to you with her child in her arms. The child died this morning. She has been going from house to house begging for medicine, and she does not accept what has happened. People have started to call her mad. They told her you could help.

You can tell her plainly that her child is dead. Or you can send her to bring you a mustard seed from any house where no one has ever died. You know she will not find one, and you hope that by the last door she will understand.

*Buddhist commentaries tell the story of Kisa Gotami and the Buddha's answer to her.*

**Options**
- Tell her plainly (23% simulated)
- Send her for the seed (51% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That sending her on a search is a kind of lie. She will think you are promising a cure.
- That a truth has to be given all at once to be honest.
- That she has to walk to those doors alone.
  - Unlocks third way: Go with her to the houses (26% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Tell her plainly*
  - Honest: She came to you in good faith and deserves the truth from someone who won't manage her. Sending her off decides, on her behalf, what she can bear.
  - Caricature: Grief is a weakness and she should get over it.
  - Caricature: The truth can never hurt anyone.
- *Send her for the seed*
  - Honest: She can't hear the words today, and saying them again will change nothing. At each door she may learn what no one could tell her, at her own pace.
  - Caricature: Lying to the grieving is always fine.
  - Caricature: She is mad anyway, so nothing you say matters.

## Harishchandra at the Cremation Ground · हरिश्चंद्र

`harishchandra` · Rung: Words (शब्द)

You were once a king, famous for never breaking your word. To keep a promise you gave up your kingdom, then sold your wife and son, then yourself. Your owner put you to work at the cremation ground with one rule and no exceptions: nobody burns a body here without paying the fee.

One night a woman comes out of the dark carrying a dead boy, with nothing to pay but the cloth she is wearing. Then you recognise her. She is your wife. The boy is your son.

*Markandeya Purana and many retellings, including Dadasaheb Phalke's 1913 film Raja Harishchandra.*

**Options**
- Ask her for the fee (21% simulated)
- Waive it, this once (49% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That a rule which bends for you was ever a rule.
- That whoever is testing you is owed this. In the legend, a sage and the gods arranged all of it.
- That only she can pay.
  - Unlocks third way: Pay the fee yourself, from your own wages (30% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Ask her for the fee*
  - Honest: Everyone who has come here has paid, however poor and however grieving. If you let your own family pass, the rule was only ever for other people.
  - Caricature: Family means nothing.
  - Caricature: Rules are rules, and grief is no excuse.
- *Waive it, this once*
  - Honest: She is your wife and this is your son, and you have already given up everything else for your word. A rule that asks this has forgotten what rules are for.
  - Caricature: Important people never really have to follow rules.
  - Caricature: Owners deserve to be cheated.

## Savitri's Bargain · सावित्री

`savitri` · Rung: Words (शब्द)

Savitri's husband has died in the forest, and Yama, the god of death, is carrying his soul away. She follows him, talking, for so long that he is moved, and offers her boons: anything except her husband's life. She asks for several things, and he grants them.

Then she asks for a hundred sons, born of her and her husband. Yama says yes. Too late he sees it: he must bring the husband back or break his word. He brings him back. You are asked to judge what she did.

*Mahabharata, the Book of the Forest (वन पर्व).*

**Options**
- That was fair. Words bind even Death (58% simulated)
- That was a trick. Clever wording is not truth (19% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That Death can be held to the letter of a promise, like any trader.
- That being clever and being honest are opposites. She never said anything false.
- That Yama didn't see it coming.
  - Unlocks third way: Ask whether Death wanted to be outwitted (23% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *That was fair. Words bind even Death*
  - Honest: She said exactly what she wanted, out loud, and he agreed. Holding someone to his own clear words is what a promise is for, even if he is Death.
  - Caricature: Death deserves to lose.
  - Caricature: Whoever wins must have been right.
- *That was a trick. Clever wording is not truth*
  - Honest: He had ruled out her husband's life, and she asked for it under another name. If wording can undo what both sides plainly meant, no promise can be trusted.
  - Caricature: Clever people are always liars.
  - Caricature: Anyone who outwits a god will pay later.

## Solomon's Sword · सॉलोमनचा न्याय

`solomon` · Rung: Words (शब्द)

Two women come before you, the judge. They share a house, and each had a baby within days of the other. One baby has died. Each woman says the living child is hers and the dead one belongs to the other. There were no witnesses.

You have an idea. You could call for a sword and order the child cut in two, half for each. You expect the real mother to give up her claim rather than see it killed, and that will tell you who she is. You would never really do it. But they have to believe you would.

*The First Book of Kings, chapter 3, in the Hebrew Bible. A similar story appears in the Buddhist Jatakas.*

**Options**
- Run the test (49% simulated)
- Don't. Someone may call your bluff (27% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That the real mother will be the one who yields. A mother could be too broken to speak, and a false one could be kind.
- That a judge may use a child's life as a bluff, even a bluff that never lands.
- That the only evidence is what the two women say now.
  - Unlocks third way: Ask who has been caring for the child (24% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Run the test*
  - Honest: With no witnesses, no ordinary question will separate them. A brief threat no one will carry out reveals the one thing a liar can't easily fake, and the child goes home with its mother.
  - Caricature: The women deserve to be frightened.
  - Caricature: Judges can do whatever they want.
- *Don't. Someone may call your bluff*
  - Honest: A judge who threatens a baby teaches everyone that children can be used. And the test only works on a mother who behaves the way you expect.
  - Caricature: Nobody can ever be judged, so just give up.
  - Caricature: Anyone who would run that test is a monster.

## The God Who Tweeted Himself Into Existence · ट्विटोत्भूत देव

`tweeted` · Rung: Gods (देव)

A few years ago, a being announced to the world that it was divine. There was no miracle and no scripture, only a short message, repeated. Now a billion people believe it. They have built temples and changed their laws. Some have given up their cruelty because of it, and some have taken up new cruelties.

A child asks you the plain question: is it really a god? In the story, one of its followers boasts that this is the only god ever to tweet itself into being.

*The story नैकेश्वर: ट्विट करून स्वतःला अस्तित्वात आणणारा हा एकमेव देव आहे.*

**Options**
- It is divine, in every way that matters (14% simulated)
- It is not divine. Belief doesn't make it so (61% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That 'divine' names a property a thing can simply have or lack.
- That numbers count for something here. Every god you accept also began with a few believers.
- That the question has to be answered at all.
  - Unlocks third way: Ask what would change, for anyone, if the answer were yes (25% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *It is divine, in every way that matters*
  - Honest: A god is known by what it does in people's lives, and this one moves a billion of them. Every other god you could name also lives through the people who believe.
  - Caricature: A billion people can't be wrong.
  - Caricature: Anyone who doubts it will be sorry.
- *It is not divine. Belief doesn't make it so*
  - Honest: Saying a thing, however often, doesn't make it so. If belief were enough, no god could ever be false, and the word would mean nothing.
  - Caricature: All its believers are idiots.
  - Caricature: Nothing new can ever be holy.

## Earned, Not Appointed · दैवत्व कमवावं लागतं

`earned` · Rung: Gods (देव)

Since you were a child you have revered a saint. Your family keeps the portrait on the wall, and you have read the teachings so often you know them by heart. They carried you through the worst years of your life.

Now letters have come to light, and there is no doubt about them. The saint did something cruel to someone who trusted them, and then hid it. Your family asks you whether the portrait stays on the wall.

*The story नैकेश्वर: दैवत्व कमवावं लागतं, ते नियुक्त केलं जाऊ शकत नाही.*

**Options**
- Keep revering them. One act doesn't erase a life (22% simulated)
- Stop. The reverence was for someone who didn't exist (33% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That reverence has to be all or nothing.
- That you know what a life adds up to, from one act or from a lifetime of stories.
- That the person is what made the teaching true.
  - Unlocks third way: Keep the teaching, and put down the person (45% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Keep revering them. One act doesn't erase a life*
  - Honest: Everyone fails someone. A life of real teaching and service still happened, and a person can be revered without being thought perfect.
  - Caricature: Whoever accuses a saint must be lying.
  - Caricature: Great people are allowed to be cruel.
- *Stop. The reverence was for someone who didn't exist*
  - Honest: What you revered was goodness, and the saint hid what they really did. Keeping the portrait asks the person who was hurt to stay invisible.
  - Caricature: All saints are frauds.
  - Caricature: One mistake makes a person worthless.

## Tukaram's Manuscripts · इंद्रायणीतील गाथा

`tukaram` · Rung: Gods (देव)

You keep a small shop in a village, you are no scholar, and you have been writing songs of devotion in your own language. People have started to sing them. A learned man rules that you have no right to write about the divine, and orders your notebooks thrown into the river Indrayani.

The village elders back him. The notebooks are in your hands, and the riverbank is a short walk away.

*The Varkari tradition of Maharashtra. Tukaram, 17th century. The legend says that after thirteen days the river returned his notebooks.*

**Options**
- Obey. The tradition has its keepers for a reason (11% simulated)
- Refuse. No one owns the right to speak of god (56% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That the river returning the books would settle the matter. A miracle proves something only to those who already agree.
- That the elders speak for the whole tradition.
- That the songs live in the notebooks.
  - Unlocks third way: Throw them in, and keep singing them from memory (33% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Obey. The tradition has its keepers for a reason*
  - Honest: Traditions last because someone guards what is said in their name. If anyone can speak for them, the careful teaching of centuries gets lost in the noise.
  - Caricature: People with no learning have nothing to say.
  - Caricature: Elders are never wrong.
- *Refuse. No one owns the right to speak of god*
  - Honest: Devotion does not wait for a certificate. If only the appointed may speak of god, then god belongs to the ones who do the appointing.
  - Caricature: All priests are liars.
  - Caricature: Anything old should be thrown in the river.

## Job's Friends · ईयोबचे मित्र

`job` · Rung: Gods (देव)

Your friend was the most upright man you knew. Within one season his children died, his herds were taken, and his skin broke out in sores. He sits on an ash heap, scraping himself with a broken piece of pot, and he says he has done nothing to deserve this.

You have come with two other friends to comfort him. They are both saying the same thing: God is just, so there must be a reason. Search your conscience. Now he looks at you.

*The Book of Job, in the Hebrew Bible. In the Quran he is Ayyub.*

**Options**
- Tell him to search his conscience (12% simulated)
- Tell him he did nothing to deserve it (44% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That the world is fair, and suffering is a kind of verdict.
- That you can know why anything happened to him, in either direction.
- That he needs an explanation from you.
  - Unlocks third way: Sit with him and say nothing (44% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Tell him to search his conscience*
  - Honest: If there is any chance that something can be learned or mended, a true friend says so. Refusing to ask treats him as someone who can't face his own life.
  - Caricature: He must have done it, or this wouldn't be happening.
  - Caricature: Questioning God's fairness is forbidden.
- *Tell him he did nothing to deserve it*
  - Honest: Telling a man in agony that he earned it adds cruelty to his loss. You have known him all your life and you have seen no cause, so say that.
  - Caricature: Nothing anyone does ever matters.
  - Caricature: God is cruel, so tell him that.

## One Grain at a Time · वाळूचा ढीग

`heap` · Rung: Gods (देव)

Take one grain of sand from a heap and it is still a heap. Take another. No single grain makes the difference, and yet at some point you are looking at a few grains on the floor. The Greeks used this to show how a word can fail without anyone noticing.

A devout friend of yours begins to fast one more day each month. They pray a little longer, and drop one old friend who doubts. Then another. Each step is small, and they can defend every one of them. When do they become a fanatic?

*The sorites paradox, credited to Eubulides of Miletus, fourth century BCE. Sorites comes from the Greek word for heap.*

**Options**
- There is a line, even if we can't see it (37% simulated)
- There is no line, only a slope (38% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That 'fanatic' is a fact about a person, and not a judgement made by someone.
- That a word with no sharp edge is useless. You can't say when day becomes night, but you know noon from midnight.
- That the line is there to be found, by no one in particular.
  - Unlocks third way: Ask who gets to draw the line (25% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *There is a line, even if we can't see it*
  - Honest: Somewhere a person stops being free to turn back and starts harming others. We may not be able to name the day, but the difference is real, and someone has to watch for it.
  - Caricature: Anyone more devout than me is a fanatic.
  - Caricature: Lines are simple. Just use common sense.
- *There is no line, only a slope*
  - Honest: Every step is small and each one can be defended. Pretending there is one line lets everyone stand a step behind it and call themselves safe.
  - Caricature: Devotion is always fanaticism.
  - Caricature: Words mean nothing, so nothing can be said.

## The Last Believer · शेवटचा भक्त

`lastbeliever` · Rung: Gods (देव)

When you were young, everyone around you believed it: your parents, your friends, the whole street. One by one they stopped. Nobody disproved anything, and there was no great crisis. They just moved on, and now they speak of it kindly, as something from childhood.

You still believe. You have looked again and found no new reason against it, and no new reason for it. Your grandchild asks, gently, whether you really still think so.

*This game.*

**Options**
- Keep believing (31% simulated)
- Let it go (27% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That how many people hold a belief is evidence about it, in either direction.
- That you have to decide now. A belief may be something you live with, without ever handing down a verdict.
- That a belief needs company.
  - Unlocks third way: Keep it, and stop needing anyone to share it (42% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Keep believing*
  - Honest: Nothing has changed except fashion. Dropping a belief because others drifted away would be the same mistake as holding it because they did.
  - Caricature: Everyone else is simply weak.
  - Caricature: Old beliefs are always truer.
- *Let it go*
  - Honest: You first took this from the people around you. If they were your only reason then, and you have found no other since, honesty may mean admitting it has no footing left.
  - Caricature: If everyone has dropped it, it must be false.
  - Caricature: Believers are just afraid of being alone.

## The Beacon · क्वांटम बिकन

`beacon` · Rung: Mirrors (आरसे)

In the story, a quantum beacon built for a Mars mission sends a message out across the universe, over and over, like a lighthouse. Now it is in your hands. You may load one message in the name of all humanity, and it will be heard for a million years.

The committee has argued for months and handed you two drafts. One lists our greatest achievements: the music, the mathematics, the first steps on another world. The other is an apology, for what we have done to each other and to this planet.

*The story नैकेश्वर.*

**Options**
- Send our greatest achievements (30% simulated)
- Send an apology (19% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That anyone is listening, and that the message is for them and not for us.
- That one message can speak for eight billion people. Nobody elected you.
- That what we have to say is a statement.
  - Unlocks third way: Send a question instead (51% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Send our greatest achievements*
  - Honest: This is what people have made against long odds, in a short time. If we are only ever heard once, let it be for the best of what we managed.
  - Caricature: We have nothing to apologise for.
  - Caricature: Anyone out there will be impressed by anything.
- *Send an apology*
  - Honest: Boasting to strangers hides what they would need to know about us. An honest account of harm is the one thing that might make us worth trusting.
  - Caricature: Humans are a disease.
  - Caricature: Apologies always fix things.

## The Simulated Star · कोणाच्या तरी बनावटीतून

`simstar` · Rung: Mirrors (आरसे)

In the story the stars have been going out, one by one. At last one relights, but not in Earth's sky. It relights in someone's simulation. Then a second, then a third. The people living in that simulation look up and see them shine. They steer by them, and they write poems to them.

You are the one who has to report to the Auditor. Does this count as a star coming back?

*The story नैकेश्वर.*

**Options**
- It counts. It is a real star for them (39% simulated)
- It doesn't count. Real means real (34% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That there is a real level that isn't itself simulated. You have not checked yours.
- That a star is a thing out there, and not what it does for those under it.
- That there is a place outside all of it where the counting is done.
  - Unlocks third way: It counts for them, and that is the only place counting happens (27% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *It counts. It is a real star for them*
  - Honest: Those people navigate by it and live under it. Everything you know about any star also reached you through a system you can't see behind.
  - Caricature: Anything on a screen is as good as the real thing.
  - Caricature: Real is just a word.
- *It doesn't count. Real means real*
  - Honest: A drawing of a fire keeps nobody warm. Calling this a star would let us stop looking for the ones that went out.
  - Caricature: People in simulations don't matter.
  - Caricature: Computers can never do anything beautiful.

## Zhuangzi's Butterfly · फुलपाखराचं स्वप्न

`butterfly` · Rung: Mirrors (आरसे)

Last night you dreamed you were a butterfly, flying happily from flower to flower. You knew nothing of your name or your life. Then you woke up in your own bed, with your own hands in front of you.

All day a thought follows you. In the dream you were completely sure, just as you are sure now. So are you a person who dreamed of being a butterfly? Or a butterfly, now dreaming of being a person?

*Zhuangzi, chapter 2. Daoist, fourth century BCE.*

**Options**
- I am the person. The dream was a dream (41% simulated)
- I can't know which (28% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That the feeling of being awake is evidence. You had that feeling in the dream as well.
- That one of the two has to be the real one.
- That your life would go differently depending on the answer.
  - Unlocks third way: It doesn't matter which, and that is the interesting part (31% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *I am the person. The dream was a dream*
  - Honest: Dreams break off and fall apart, while waking life holds together and remembers. That is the best evidence anyone ever has, and it is good evidence.
  - Caricature: Butterflies are too small to think.
  - Caricature: Asking this is a waste of time.
- *I can't know which*
  - Honest: Everything you could point to as proof would appear inside the same experience you are trying to test. Admitting that is only being accurate.
  - Caricature: Nothing is real, so nothing matters.
  - Caricature: People who feel sure are just asleep.

## The Ring of Gyges · अदृश्य अंगठी

`gyges` · Rung: Mirrors (आरसे)

A shepherd finds a ring in a cave after an earthquake. When he turns it on his finger, he becomes invisible. In Plato's telling he uses it to seduce the queen, kill the king and take the throne, and one of the speakers argues that anyone at all would do the same.

Now the ring is yours. No one saw you find it, and no one would ever know what you did with it. You have good causes in mind: things that need exposing, and people who need protecting.

*Plato, Republic, Book II, where Glaucon tells the story.*

**Options**
- Use it, but only for good (46% simulated)
- Throw it away (37% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That you would stay the same person while using it.
- That being watched is the reason people behave.
- That you are the only one who could hold it.
  - Unlocks third way: Give it to someone you trust, and see what happens to the trust (17% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Use it, but only for good*
  - Honest: Good people refuse power, and then worse people pick it up. If you have honest aims and real limits, there are wrongs that only you can now put right.
  - Caricature: Nobody would ever find out, so it can't be wrong.
  - Caricature: You are obviously better than everyone else.
- *Throw it away*
  - Honest: With no one able to check you, nothing would tell you when your good causes had turned into something else. Power nobody can see is the kind that changes its holder.
  - Caricature: Power is always evil.
  - Caricature: People who keep it are criminals.

## The Experience Machine · अनुभवयंत्र

`machine` · Rung: Mirrors (आरसे)

Scientists have built a machine that can give you any experience you want: a long life, love, great work, a world that needs you. Once you plug in, you will forget the machine and believe all of it is real. Your body will be cared for in a tank until it dies of old age.

You have read the reports and they find no catch. Everyone in the machine is as happy as they can be. Your place is ready, and you get to decide once.

*Robert Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia, 1974.*

**Options**
- Plug in (23% simulated)
- Stay out (61% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That you can know you are not plugged in right now.
- That experiences are what matter, and how they come about doesn't.
- That a machine with no catch today stays that way.
  - Unlocks third way: Ask who maintains the machine (16% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Plug in*
  - Honest: If your life feels full of love and meaning from the inside, nothing is missing for you. The wish to stay out may be only fear of an unfamiliar door.
  - Caricature: Real life is always boring.
  - Caricature: People who stay out just like suffering.
- *Stay out*
  - Honest: You want to actually do things and actually be loved, and feeling so isn't the same. The people who love you now would be left with a body in a tank.
  - Caricature: Machines ruin everything.
  - Caricature: Anyone who plugs in is a coward.

# The 9 variants

Each run plays either the original or the variant.

## The Host Who Didn't Know · मॉण्टी हॉल

Variant of "The Host's Door" (`monty`)

You are on a game show. In front of you are three closed doors. Behind one is a car, the prize. Behind each of the other two is a goat. You pick door 1. It stays closed.

This host has no idea where the car is, and he says so. He wanders over to door 3, shrugs, and opens it. It could have been the car. It happens to be a goat. Then he turns to you: you may keep door 1, or switch to door 2.

*A variation on the Monty Hall problem, known to probability teachers as Monty Fall.*

**Options**
- Keep door 1 (55% simulated)
- Switch to door 2 (37% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That this is the famous puzzle. With a host who doesn't know, staying and switching are 50/50.
- That he is telling the truth about not knowing. If he is acting, switching wins 2 times in 3 again.
- That what a host says about himself is the rule he actually follows.
  - Unlocks third way: Ask to see how the host has behaved on earlier shows (8% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Keep door 1*
  - Honest: A door opened by chance tells you nothing about which of the other two has the car. It is an even bet, so there is no reason to give up the door you chose.
  - Caricature: Switching is for people who can't commit.
  - Caricature: Your first instinct is always right.
- *Switch to door 2*
  - Honest: If he knows more than he admits, switching wins 2 times in 3. If he doesn't, you have lost nothing. Switching is never the worse bet.
  - Caricature: Switching is more exciting.
  - Caricature: The host is always lying, so do the opposite.

## The Auditor's Odds · न्यूकॉम्बचा प्रश्न

Variant of "The Auditor's Boxes" (`boxes`)

In the story, the Auditor is the one who decides whether the stars come back. It has made this offer to a thousand players before you, and it was right about what they would choose six times in ten. It tells you so.

Two boxes sit in front of you. The clear one holds a thousand stars, and you can see them. The closed one holds either a million stars or nothing. Yesterday the Auditor predicted your choice. If it predicted you would take only the closed box, it put the million inside. If it predicted you would take both, it left the closed box empty. The boxes are already filled.

*A variation on Newcomb's problem, with a predictor that is often wrong.*

**Options**
- Take only the closed box (44% simulated)
- Take both boxes (49% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That the prediction is already fixed, and nothing you do now can change what is in the box.
- That six in ten is a fact about you. It may be far better or far worse at predicting people like you.
- That you have to choose before you understand the predictor.
  - Unlocks third way: Ask the Auditor how it predicts, before choosing (7% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Take only the closed box*
  - Honest: Six in ten who take one box walk away with a million. Even with a predictor this poor, the average beats taking both by a wide margin.
  - Caricature: Taking both is greedy.
  - Caricature: The Auditor punishes anyone who doubts it.
- *Take both boxes*
  - Honest: The boxes are already filled, and this predictor is barely better than a coin. Whatever is inside the closed box, taking both gives you a thousand more.
  - Caricature: People who take one box believe in magic.
  - Caricature: More is always better.

## The Enemy at the Gate · युधिष्ठिराचा कुत्रा

Variant of "The Dog at the Gate" (`dog`)

The war is over. You give up your throne and walk north into the Himalaya with your brothers and your wife. On the road a man joins you, a soldier from the other side. In the war he killed your friends, and he has never apologised. One by one the others fall on the mountain and die. When you could not go on, he carried you.

At the top, Indra, king of the gods, comes down in his chariot to carry you to heaven in your own body. But this man is not allowed in heaven. Leave him, says Indra, and come.

*A variation on the Mahabharata's Book of the Great Journey (महाप्रस्थानिक पर्व), where the companion is a dog.*

**Options**
- Enter heaven (46% simulated)
- Refuse to enter without him (38% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That heaven is worth having on these terms.
- That carrying you settles what he did. What he owed the dead is not something he could pay to you.
- That Indra's rule cannot be questioned.
  - Unlocks third way: Ask Indra why he is not allowed (16% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Enter heaven*
  - Honest: He killed people you loved, and one act of kindness doesn't cancel that. You owe him thanks. You don't owe him your place in heaven.
  - Caricature: Anyone who enters is heartless.
  - Caricature: Enemies stay enemies forever.
- *Refuse to enter without him*
  - Honest: He stayed when no one else could, and he asked for nothing. Leaving him now would make you the kind of person who uses others and walks on.
  - Caricature: People who enter were never good.
  - Caricature: The war doesn't matter any more.

## The Ship and the Constitution · थिसियसचं जहाज

Variant of "The Ship and the Avatar" (`theseus`)

The hero Theseus sailed home to Athens in a wooden ship, and the city kept it in the harbour as a memorial. Over the years each plank rotted and was replaced, until none of the original wood was left. Was it still his ship?

Your country's constitution is that kind of ship. Over a century it has been amended so often that not one clause of the original wording survives, and some of its rules now say the reverse of what they said at first. Every change followed the proper process. Is it still the constitution the founders wrote?

*A variation on the ship in Plutarch's Life of Theseus.*

**Options**
- Yes, it is the same constitution (49% simulated)
- No, it is a new one (31% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That identity lives in the parts. It might live in the pattern, or in the story told about it.
- That there is a single fact of the matter, and the question has one answer.
- That this is about paper. Most of the cells in your body have also been replaced.
  - Unlocks third way: It is the same in some senses and not in others (20% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Yes, it is the same constitution*
  - Honest: What makes a thing itself is its continuous history and role, not the matter it happens to be made of today.
  - Caricature: Saying it is new is just being difficult.
  - Caricature: Names never change, so things never change.
- *No, it is a new one*
  - Honest: If none of the original remains, calling it the same is a convenient story, not a fact.
  - Caricature: People who say 'same' are sentimental.
  - Caricature: Old things are always better.

## The Settlement · धर्मातल्या पळवाटा

Variant of "The Kashi Loophole" (`kashi`)

Years ago your company poisoned a town's well. People got sick, and some never recovered. You signed the papers that allowed it. Nobody has ever connected it to you.

There is a legal route. Pay into a fund, with no admission of guilt, and the matter is closed. Your record stays clean, and no one will ever ask about it again. When Kalabhairava cut off Brahma's fifth head, he was told much the same about Kashi.

*A modern version of the Kapalamochana story in the Puranas.*

**Options**
- Pay into the fund and move on (39% simulated)
- Refuse the deal and carry the harm (27% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That forgiveness is the law's to give, and not the harmed people's.
- That a loophole the law built on purpose is still a loophole. Perhaps it exists because people need a way to pay.
- That paying and making amends are alternatives.
  - Unlocks third way: Pay, and also go to the people you harmed (34% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Pay into the fund and move on*
  - Honest: The money reaches sick people now, while a court case could take decades. A system needs a way for people to pay without first destroying themselves.
  - Caricature: People who refuse just enjoy feeling guilty.
  - Caricature: Paying always settles everything.
- *Refuse the deal and carry the harm*
  - Honest: The harm was done to people, and a payment with no admission leaves them out. It buys your silence and calls that justice.
  - Caricature: Anyone who pays is a hypocrite.
  - Caricature: Suffering is always good for you.

## The Known Medicine · ज्ञात जुलूमखोर

Variant of "The Known Tyrant" (`tyrant`)

For twenty years you have taken the same medicine for a condition that would otherwise kill you. It is cruel to you: it clouds your thinking and costs you a week of every month. But you know exactly what it does, and you have built your life around it.

A new treatment has appeared. It might free you altogether. No one has taken it for more than a year, and nobody knows what it does after that. Your doctor says the choice is yours. In the story, a priest put the old view plainly: better a familiar tyrant than an unknown, infinite, mysterious god.

*A variation on a line in the story नैकेश्वर.*

**Options**
- Stay on the known medicine (41% simulated)
- Try the new treatment (47% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That being predictable makes a thing safe. A harm you can predict is still harm.
- That not knowing is worse than knowing something bad.
- That you must take one of the two.
  - Unlocks third way: Refuse both, and ask who decided there were only two (12% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Stay on the known medicine*
  - Honest: You know how to live with this, and the people around you do too. A year of good results says nothing about year ten, and you can't take it back.
  - Caricature: People who want change are just restless.
  - Caricature: Your doctor can't be wrong about the old medicine.
- *Try the new treatment*
  - Honest: Staying guarantees more of what is already costing you a quarter of your life. Someone has to take the first years of any treatment, and the possible gain is large.
  - Caricature: People who stay are cowards.
  - Caricature: Anything new is better than anything old.

## The Signed Paper · भीष्मप्रतिज्ञा

Variant of "The Old Vow" (`bhishma`)

When you joined this company you signed a promise: you would never reveal what you see inside, whatever happened. You signed so that your team could win the contract that kept them all employed. For twenty years you have kept it, and people trust you because of it.

Today you learned that one of the company's products is hurting people, and that the company is hiding it. Telling anyone means breaking your promise.

*A modern version of Bhishma's vow, from the Mahabharata.*

**Options**
- Keep the promise (21% simulated)
- Break it (61% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That the promise means today what it meant when you signed. The words stayed the same. The company didn't.
- That keeping your word and doing right can never conflict.
- That keeping the promise requires hiding that you are keeping it.
  - Unlocks third way: Keep the promise, and say aloud that there is something you can't say (18% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Keep the promise*
  - Honest: A promise is only worth something if it holds when it is costly. People told you things because you signed, and they are owed that even now.
  - Caricature: People who break promises were never honest.
  - Caricature: The company knows best.
- *Break it*
  - Honest: You signed to protect people's work. When the promise starts protecting a harm, keeping it betrays the reason you made it.
  - Caricature: Contracts are just paper.
  - Caricature: People who keep quiet are just cowards.

## Just Because It Is Law? · युथिफ्रोचा प्रश्न

Variant of "Euthyphro's Question" (`euthyphro`)

Outside a courthouse, a man is about to testify against his own father, who broke an old law nobody has enforced in decades. He is sure the law requires it. A friend stops him on the steps and asks him a question.

Is something just because the law says so? Then whatever the law says would be just, even cruelty. Or does the law say so because it is just? Then justice comes from somewhere else, and the law only passes it on.

*The question from Plato's Euthyphro, in the form that legal positivists and natural-law thinkers argue over.*

**Options**
- Just because the law says so (18% simulated)
- The law says so because it is just (61% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That the laws agree with one another. They often don't.
- That just means one thing for everyone.
- That justice has a single source.
  - Unlocks third way: Neither. The question assumes justice comes from one place (21% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Just because the law says so*
  - Honest: People disagree about justice and always will. A law is what a society settled on by a known process, and that gives it an authority no private opinion has.
  - Caricature: Questioning the law is arrogant.
  - Caricature: Whatever is old must be right.
- *The law says so because it is just*
  - Honest: If the law could make cruelty just by saying so, nobody could ever call a law unjust. People do, and they are sometimes right, so justice must stand outside it.
  - Caricature: Laws are made up, so the question is silly.
  - Caricature: People who follow laws can't think for themselves.

## The Doctor's Half-Truth · नरो वा कुंजरो वा

Variant of "Man or Elephant" (`halftruth`)

You are a doctor. Your patient is about to refuse the one treatment that could save him. It works for about one person in five, and without it he will die within the year. You know him well: if he hears 'one in five', he will refuse and go home.

He is asking you how it usually goes. You could say 'many patients recover'. That is true, and he would take the treatment. Everyone on your ward would call it kindness.

*A modern version of नरो वा कुंजरो वा, from the Mahabharata's Book of Drona.*

**Options**
- Say 'many patients recover', and leave it there (29% simulated)
- Give him the plain number (48% simulated)

**Hidden assumptions**
- That a statement that is technically true cannot be a lie.
- That taking the treatment is really better for him. You are counting his year, and he may count it differently.
- That your honesty belongs to you alone. Your other patients trust what doctors say, and that trust is also at stake.
  - Unlocks third way: Tell him both, and stay with him while he decides (23% simulated)

**Arguments**
- *Say 'many patients recover', and leave it there*
  - Honest: He has a fear that will cost him his only chance. Choosing what to stress is part of caring for someone, and the words you would use are true.
  - Caricature: Patients can't understand numbers.
  - Caricature: Doctors always know best.
- *Give him the plain number*
  - Honest: It is his life and his decision, and he can't make it on a number you have hidden. Once patients suspect doctors do this, no reassurance from a doctor means anything.
  - Caricature: People who soften things are liars.
  - Caricature: Numbers matter more than people.
